The short answer

First separate astronomy from convention

A Panchang combines calculated astronomical positions with traditional rules for organizing a local day and choosing observances. The Sun and Moon positions are calculated for a moment, but sunrise, local clock time and the rule applied to a transition determine how those positions are presented.

Place changes the clockLongitude, latitude and timezone affect local sunrise, sunset and the clock time of a transition.
The day is often sunrise-basedA Tithi present at local sunrise may be used as the day’s headline even when it ends later that morning.
Observance rules varyFestival and Vrat decisions may use sunrise, moonrise, midday, Pradosh or another required period—not simply midnight.

Daily timing

Why Rahu Kaal and Muhurta move by location

Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda and Gulika are derived from the local daylight span. As sunrise and sunset change with place and season, their clock times move too. Abhijit Muhurta is likewise tied to the local daytime midpoint. A memorized weekday table is only a rough shortcut; a city-aware result is more useful.

Tithi and Nakshatra transitions are astronomical events, but the displayed local time still depends on timezone. A transition close to sunrise can also affect which value is emphasized for the day.

Practical rule: Use the city where the activity will occur. For a festival or family observance, also confirm the regional rule followed by your tradition.

Next step

Use the result with the right level of certainty

For ordinary daily reference, use the local Panchang and read the five limbs together. For a festival, Samskara or consequential Muhurta, record the city, date and convention used so the choice can be reviewed consistently.
